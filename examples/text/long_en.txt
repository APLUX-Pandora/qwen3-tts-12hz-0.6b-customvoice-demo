The Lighthouse Keeper

On a narrow island at the edge of the northern sea, there was a lighthouse that had stood for more than two hundred years. Its walls were thick and white, its stairs were made of iron, and at the very top a great lamp turned slowly through the night. For most of its long life the lighthouse had been tended by a single keeper, and in the year this story begins that keeper was a quiet woman named Margaret Hale.

Margaret had come to the island when she was young. Her father had been the keeper before her, and she had learned the work by watching him. Every evening she climbed the one hundred and twelve steps, cleaned the glass, checked the oil, and lit the lamp. Every morning she climbed them again, put the light out, and wrote a few lines in the logbook. The entries were short. "Wind from the west. Heavy rain after midnight. One fishing boat passed at dawn." Over the years the logbook had grown into a long, patient record of weather and ships.

People on the mainland sometimes asked her whether she was lonely. She always answered in the same way. "I have the sea, the birds, and the light," she would say. "And every ship that passes is a kind of visitor." It was true that she rarely spoke to anyone for days at a time. Yet she did not feel alone. She knew the moods of the water as well as other people knew the faces of their neighbours.

Once a month a small supply boat came from the harbour town. It was usually steered by an old sailor called Mr. Thomas Reed, who brought flour, coffee, lamp oil, newspapers, and letters. Mr. Reed liked to talk. He told her about the price of fish, the new school, the mayor's arguments with the council, and the weddings and funerals of people she had known as a child. Margaret listened, nodded, and asked careful questions. When the boat left, she would stand on the rocks and wave until it disappeared behind the headland.

In the autumn of that year, the weather turned strange. Storms arrived without warning, and the wind changed direction several times in a single afternoon. The barometer in the kitchen fell so quickly that Margaret tapped it twice, thinking it was broken. It was not broken. That night the sea rose higher than she had ever seen it, and the waves struck the lower windows of the tower with a sound like distant cannons.

At about two o'clock in the morning, she saw a faint light moving on the water to the south. At first she thought it was a reflection. Then she saw it again, lower and closer, swinging from side to side. It was a ship, and it was much too near the reef. Margaret did not hesitate. She checked that the great lamp was turning at full strength, and then she went to the small signal lamp that her father had kept for emergencies. She began to flash the old warning code, slowly and clearly, again and again.

For a long time nothing happened. The wind screamed around the gallery, and rain ran down the glass in thick, grey sheets. Her hands grew cold and stiff, but she kept flashing the signal. Finally the moving light stopped swinging. It turned, very slowly, away from the reef and toward the deeper water in the east. Margaret watched it until it became a small, steady point, and then until it vanished completely.

The next morning the storm was over. The sky was pale and clean, and the sea was calm, as if nothing had happened. Margaret wrote in the logbook as she always did. "Severe storm from the south west. Unknown vessel near the reef at two o'clock. Warning given. Vessel changed course." Then she made a pot of coffee and sat by the window, too tired to sleep.

Three weeks later, the supply boat arrived with an unusual passenger. A tall man in a dark coat climbed onto the rocks behind Mr. Reed and asked to speak to the keeper. He introduced himself as Dr. Samuel Brooks, the captain's brother. He explained that the ship she had warned was a cargo vessel carrying grain, medicine, and twenty three crew members. Its radio had failed in the storm, and its captain had lost his position. "If you had not been watching," he said, "we would have lost the ship and everyone on it."

Margaret did not know what to say. She offered him coffee and showed him the logbook. He read the short entry several times and smiled. "You wrote it as if it were an ordinary night," he said. Margaret shrugged. "It was my work," she answered. "The light is there so that people can come home."

Before he left, Dr. Brooks gave her a letter from the whole crew. They had all signed it, some with careful handwriting and some with large, uneven letters. The letter said only a few words. "Thank you for keeping the light." Margaret put it inside the logbook, between the pages of that stormy autumn night.

Years later, when the lighthouse was finally made automatic and the last keeper was no longer needed, Margaret moved to a small house in the harbour town. She kept the old logbooks on a shelf in her kitchen. Children from the school sometimes came to visit her, and she would show them the pages, the weather, the ships, and the letter. "A light is a simple thing," she told them. "But someone has to care enough to keep it burning."
